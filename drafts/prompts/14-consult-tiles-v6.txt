Editorial illustration, 16:9 landscape.

REFERENCE IMAGES — Two reference pictures are attached. Take ONE thing from each and
nothing else.
  • THE PHOTOGRAPH OF A CHIPPED CROWN — take ONLY how the porcelain has broken off a tooth:
    a large irregular area of exposed DARK GREY metal covering most of the front of the
    tooth, with just a narrow rim of pale porcelain surviving around its edges. Do NOT copy
    anything else — not the photographic look, not the mouth, lips, gums or staining.
  • THE DRAWING OF A CLINIC CONSULTATION ROOM — take ONLY the kind of room and its
    furniture: ring binders standing in rows on shelving, a pale desk, wheeled office
    chairs, window blinds, a framed picture, a potted plant. Do NOT copy its colours —
    that room is pink and warm; this one follows the COLOUR section below. Do NOT copy how
    it is drawn or how empty it is: BOTH reference pictures supply CONTENT ONLY, never the
    drawing style and never the amount of stuff in the room. The finished picture must look
    like the STYLE section below, and its room must be far fuller than the reference.

STYLE — THIS MATTERS MORE THAN ANY OTHER SECTION.
Contemporary printed-magazine editorial illustration, hand-made throughout. Linework in
warm dark brown or soft charcoal, NEVER pure black: thin, hand-drawn, the weight visibly
varies along a stroke, strokes taper and sometimes break and run dry at the end — never an
even mechanical vector line. Colour applied like soft coloured pencil and light marker;
colour edges a little loose and not always meeting the line. Flat fills with two or three
tones per hue — no smooth decorative gradients, no airbrush, no glow. A fine even paper
grain over every surface of the whole image.
SKIN IS THE ONE EXCEPTION to the two-or-three-tones rule: each face and each hand is ONE
single flat tone, with no shading, no cheekbones and no jawline. On a face, draw ONLY these
six things plus blush: the outline of the head, the eyes, the eyebrows, the nose, the mouth,
the ears — and TWO SOFT ROUND PATCHES OF WARM PINK BLUSH, one high on each cheek, on every
single person in the picture. The blush is part of the house style and must be drawn; what
is banned is wrinkles, a line beside the nose, a line under the eye, cheek shadow and any
modelling of the face.
THE PICTURE IS BUSY AND LIVED-IN, not a minimal showroom: every part of the frame has
something real in it, surfaces carry objects, and there are more people than just the two
in the foreground.
HAIR — the two clinic staff and the patient have DIFFERENT hair, and both must stay very
dark:
  • THE DENTIST AND THE ASSISTANT (both in scrubs) have BLUE-BLACK hair — #222d39, its
    shadowed side #171e27, its lit edge #344151. This is black hair with a cool blue sheen
    in the light, the way dark Asian hair looks under a window; it must stay nearly black.
    If in doubt, draw it DARKER.
  • THE PATIENT has warm dark brown hair — #3f3330, its shadowed side #2e2523, its lit edge
    #4d403c.
NEVER grey, silver, white, ash or steel hair on anyone, and never a pale or mid blue-grey:
the staff's hair is dark enough to read as black at a glance, never as greying hair.

STRUCTURE — ONE single illustration, no panels and no dividing lines. A dental clinic
consultation room. THE DENTIST SITS ON THE RIGHT SIDE OF THE DESK and the PATIENT SITS ON
THE LEFT SIDE, facing each other. ONE large rounded-rectangle speech bubble floats in the
UPPER RIGHT of the frame, directly ABOVE THE DENTIST, and its short tapering tail comes
down out of the bubble and points at HER — at the dentist's head, on the right. THE BUBBLE
IS HERS: she is the one explaining. The tail must never reach across the picture towards
the patient, the bubble must never sit above or behind the patient, and there is never more
than one bubble and never an arrow.

THE MODEL IN HER HANDS — the first thing the reader must see. She holds up a DENTAL
DEMONSTRATION MODEL: a hinged model of an upper and a lower jaw with a full row of teeth in
each, held open a little so both rows are clearly visible, resting on her palms and lifted
to about chest height, turned towards the patient but angled so the viewer sees the teeth
too. DRAW IT LARGE — about as big as both her hands together, roughly the size of her head
— so that individual teeth are clearly readable. This scale is deliberate and must not be
corrected. The teeth are clean ivory white; the base holding them is a plain pale beige,
not pink and not red.
THREE OF THE TEETH ON THIS MODEL ARE BROKEN CROWNS, and they are the point of the picture.
Put them in three separate places, well apart from one another — one near the front of the
upper row, one further back on the other side of the upper row, and one in the lower row.
Each of the three is drawn exactly as in the attached photograph: most of the front of the
tooth is a large irregular patch of DARK GREY METAL, with only a narrow rim of pale
porcelain left around its edges, and a clearly drawn irregular boundary between the two
where the porcelain shows as a thin layer seen edge-on. The dark metal must be strongly
dark against the ivory teeth around it, so that at a glance the model reads as a row of
clean white teeth with three dark bites taken out of it. Every other tooth on the model is
whole and clean. Two small pale porcelain flakes lie on the desk below.

THE BUBBLE — Inside it, drawn simply and cleanly: A SHORT ROW OF THREE ORDINARY TAIWANESE
TOWNHOUSES JOINED SIDE BY SIDE, seen from across the street at a slight upward angle, five
or six storeys, with a road and a pavement running along the bottom of the bubble in front
of them — a kerb, a scooter parked at the edge, a couple of street trees. They are a
terrace, not one lone tower standing by itself.
THE MIDDLE HOUSE is the one being talked about. Its facade is faced with SMALL SQUARE WALL
TILES in a regular grid — pale warm off-white tiles, crossed by TWO OR THREE HORIZONTAL
BANDS of tiles in #335b8b running the full width between floors, the way Taiwanese
apartment blocks use a second tile colour as a stripe. Plain window openings, a rooftop
water tank, a few potted plants and a laundry pole on a balcony. DO NOT draw window grilles
or security bars.
Its tiles have been falling off for years, shown THREE ways at once:
  • ONE LARGE CONTINUOUS PATCH high on the wall, about the size of a window, tiles
    completely gone, the rough darker grey cement bed exposed, a ragged fringe of
    half-attached tiles around its edge;
  • THIRTY OR FORTY SINGLE TILES MISSING scattered irregularly over the rest of the facade,
    some alone, some in twos and threes, heaviest near the large patch and thinning lower
    down, so the wall reads speckled and pitted, like a picture with pixels knocked out;
  • THREE OR FOUR TILES CAUGHT IN MID-AIR falling away below the large patch, tumbling at
    different angles — it is still happening right now.
The two neighbouring houses are ordinary and undamaged, in different plain wall colours,
so the middle one stands out. Faint pale rain-streaks run down from the gaps. On the
pavement below the middle house: fallen tiles, two snapped in half, TWO ORANGE-AND-WHITE
TRAFFIC CONES and a simple plain barrier rail between them. The houses are weathered but
plainly still lived in — NOT a ruin, NOT abandoned, NOT collapsing; no structural crack, no
scaffolding, nobody standing underneath, nobody in danger.
KEEP THE BUILDINGS INSIDE THE BUBBLE and the teeth outside it: no tiles anywhere in the
room, no tooth shape anywhere inside the bubble.

THE TWO PEOPLE — THE DENTIST, on the RIGHT, a woman in her thirties with short hair, seen
three-quarters from the front, sitting upright and holding up the model. She wears an OPEN
WHITE CLINICAL COAT over her scrubs — a proper doctor's coat with a collar, long sleeves
and a chest pocket, worn open so the scrubs show at the front, the collar and the cuffs.
Her expression is friendly, attentive and matter-of-fact — she is explaining an ordinary
thing, not delivering bad news — and she looks towards the patient.
THE PATIENT, on the LEFT, a man of about forty-five, TURNED SO WE CLEARLY SEE HIS FACE,
three-quarters towards the viewer, looking at the model. HIS EXPRESSION MATTERS: he is
TAKEN ABACK — mildly astonished that such a thing happens at all. Both eyebrows raised and
level, eyes open a little wider than usual, mouth slightly open, one hand come a few inches
off the desk with the palm turned upward in a small unfinished gesture. Surprised and
interested — NOT frightened, NOT upset, NOT in pain, NOT comical, NOT open-mouthed in
shock. Neither of them looks at the viewer.

THE ROOM — A REAL DENTAL CLINIC CONSULTATION OFFICE, not a domestic dining room. It must
NOT look homely: no wooden dining table, no wooden dining chairs, no rug, no wooden
floorboards, no kitchen furniture. Instead: a pale smooth desk with a plain rounded edge;
two wheeled office chairs with low backs; behind the dentist, open shelving with a row of
upright ring binders and a couple of plain storage boxes; a slim window with pale venetian
blinds on the left giving clean daylight; ONE framed picture on the wall whose image is
fixed and must be exactly this — soft rounded hills in three flat bands of colour, nothing
else; a small potted plant; a pale smooth floor. NO computer monitor on the desk — the desk
in front of them is clear apart from the two porcelain flakes. Do NOT add any poster,
chart, diagram, X-ray, plaster model, screen or picture of teeth or buildings to the room.
THE ROOM MUST BE FULL — this is the single biggest thing to get right about it. Behind and
around the desk, all of this is visible and none of it may be left out: the corner of a
dental chair with its headrest and its overhead operating lamp on a jointed arm, over on
the right; a low counter with a small sink; on that counter, five or six small ordinary
things (a stack of paper cups, a tissue box, a small instrument tray, two or three little
jars, a bottle of hand gel); a SECOND MEMBER OF STAFF in the background — a young dental
assistant in the same blue scrubs, standing at that counter with her back three-quarters to
us, quietly tidying an instrument tray, not looking at anyone; three potted plants of
different sizes, one on the floor, one on the shelf, one on the counter; a small wall clock
with a blank face; a pale tiled floor with visible tile joints. The room should feel like a
clinic that is being used right now.
THREE THINGS IN IT ARE PAINTED THE DEEP BLUE #335b8b, and they must
all three actually be drawn — they are what ties the room to the rest of the picture, and a
room without them is wrong:
  • A BROAD HORIZONTAL PAINTED BAND runs along the wall behind the two people at about desk
    height, from one edge of the frame to the other, roughly a hand's width tall — a solid
    band of #335b8b, like the painted dado you see in a clinic corridor. The wall above it
    and below it stays pale.
  • THE CLOSED CUPBOARD DOORS in the lower half of the shelving unit behind the dentist are
    solid #335b8b, with plain slim handles.
  • THE SEAT CUSHIONS AND BACKRESTS of both wheeled office chairs are upholstered in the
    same #335b8b.
Everything else in the room — the walls above and below the band, the desk, the floor, the
blinds, the shelf frame, the ring binders — stays pale and warm. The blue is on those three
things and nowhere else in the room.

PEOPLE — Simplified illustrative people: minimal features (small dot or short-line eyes, a
simple line mouth, a small nose), natural realistic head-to-body proportions, believable
ages, East Asian faces. Not photorealistic. Not faceless. No oversized heads, no noodle
limbs. Hands are drawn at a normal size, never splayed and never larger than a face.
CLOTHING — the dentist: an open white clinical coat over scrubs in #6689b2, the scrubs'
folds and shadows in #4c6f9a — a deep, clearly saturated blue, NOT a pale powder blue and
NOT washed out. The patient wears a muted clay-rose knitted top with warm grey trousers — a
clearly different colour family. Clothes are NEVER flat single-tone shapes: model every
garment with two or three tones of its own colour, with soft coloured-pencil shading in the
folds, and draw the real details — collar, cuff, sleeve seam, hem, a pocket.

LIGHT AND COLOUR — Ordinary bright indoor daytime, ONE light source: clean daylight through
the blinds on the left, warm and even. No long orange shadows, not sunset, not lamplight,
no spotlight on the model. THE PICTURE NEEDS MOVEMENT IN IT — draw all three of these:
  • DAYLIGHT COMING IN. From the window at the left, SIX OR SEVEN long white hand-drawn
    rays fan out diagonally across the upper part of the room, all running the same way,
    widening as they cross the frame, solid where they start and thinning to dry flecks —
    soft chalk quality, clearly hand-made. Draw them boldly enough to be plainly visible,
    not as three faint hairlines. They pass above and behind the people; they never begin
    at anyone's mouth, nose, hand or body, never cross a face, never loop or curl back on
    themselves, and never resemble smoke, steam or breath.
  • THE FALLING TILES ARE MOVING. Behind each tile falling through the air inside the
    bubble, draw TWO OR THREE SHORT straight motion strokes trailing upward in the
    direction it came from, all parallel, short and light — the ordinary comic mark for
    something dropping. Never one long curved ribbon.
  • THE PATIENT'S GESTURE IS ALIVE. His raised hand is clearly lifted off the desk, palm up
    and fingers slightly spread, caught in the middle of a movement rather than resting.
THIS PICTURE MUST NOT GO GREY. Most coloured areas sit in the
range HSL saturation 30–50 and lightness 70–85, and at least half the picture is chromatic.
The room is warm, not clinical-cold: alongside the blue, use soft mint green, pale butter
yellow, muted lilac and warm rose on the small things — the ring binders on the shelf are
several different colours, the paper cups, the tissue box, the little jars, the plant pots,
the folded towels and the framed hills are each a different one. Use AT LEAST SEVEN clearly
different colours across the picture and give each to a named thing: the scrubs, the
patient's knitted top, the room wall, the binders, the plant pots, the counter items, the
framed hills, the sky and street trees inside the bubble, the neighbouring houses' walls.
ACCENT — inside the bubble, the same blue #335b8b is used for the horizontal tile bands on
the middle house, so the building and the room belong to one family. Together with the
three painted things listed in THE ROOM, that is everywhere this blue appears. Do NOT tint
the skin, the model, the desk, the floor, the sky or the paper with it. There is NO pink,
red or warm rose anywhere except the patient's top.

CRITICAL — NO WRITING ANYWHERE IN THE IMAGE. No text, letters, words, numbers, logos, shop
signs, house numbers, number plates, labels on the ring binders, captions or watermarks, in
any language or alphabet. The ring binder spines are completely blank. Inside the bubble
every shopfront, shutter and wall is blank. The traffic cones and barrier carry no writing.
There are no arrows, ticks, crosses, plus or minus signs or dotted lines anywhere. Where
writing would normally appear, leave the surface plain.

REMEMBER THE STYLE — before anything else, this is a soft hand-drawn coloured-pencil
editorial illustration with visible paper grain, hand-drawn lines of varying weight, warm
varied colour and blushing cheeks on every face, and a room full of real objects and a
third person working in the background. It is NOT a clean flat vector diagram, NOT a sparse
minimal room, and NOT a copy of the attached reference drawings.

AVOID — greyscale; a picture that reads as mostly grey; flat clean vector artwork; an empty
or sparse room; only two people in the whole picture; faces without blush; bare surfaces
with nothing on them; an all-white, colourless
consultation room; a room where the wall band, the cupboard doors or the chair upholstery
have been left pale instead of blue; every surface in the room turned blue; photorealism; a
photorealistic mouth, gum or hand; faceless or noodle-limbed figures; thick uniform black
outlines; even mechanical vector linework; chrome, metallic or iridescent gradients; glow,
sparkle or lens flare; grey, silver, white, ash or steel hair; the clinic staff's hair
drawn pale, mid-toned or grey-blue instead of nearly black; the patient's hair drawn blue;
wrinkles, cheek shadow or facial modelling; the bubble placed above or behind the patient; the bubble's tail pointing
at the patient or crossing the picture; the dentist on the left and the patient on the
right; the patient drawn from behind or with his face hidden; a frightened, upset, pained
or cartoonishly shocked patient; the dentist without a white coat; pale powder-blue scrubs;
the model drawn small or held in two fingers like a single tooth; a single loose tooth
instead of a jaw model; only one broken tooth, or all the broken teeth bunched together in
one spot; pink or red gums on the model; the exposed metal drawn pale, silver, shiny or the
same colour as the porcelain; a homely dining room, wooden dining table, wooden dining
chairs, rug or wooden floorboards; ONE lone building standing by itself inside the bubble;
window grilles or security bars; a computer monitor; more than one bubble; any arrow,
pointer or comparison mark; a ruined, abandoned or collapsing building, structural cracks,
scaffolding, workers, an accident, an injured person, blood, an ambulance, flashing lights
or barrier tape; people standing under the falling tiles; blood, wounds, redness or a mouth
anywhere; any tooth shape inside the bubble; any tile anywhere in the room; anyone looking
at the viewer; flat untextured single-tone clothing; a bare empty room.
