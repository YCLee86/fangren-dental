Editorial illustration, 16:9 landscape.

STYLE — READ THIS FIRST; IT MATTERS MORE THAN ANY OTHER SECTION.
Contemporary printed-magazine editorial illustration, hand-made throughout. Linework in
warm dark brown or soft charcoal, NEVER pure black: thin, hand-drawn, the weight visibly
varies along a stroke, strokes taper and sometimes break and run dry at the end — never an
even mechanical vector line. Colour applied like soft coloured pencil and light marker;
colour edges a little loose and not always meeting the line. Flat fills with two or three
tones per hue — no smooth decorative gradients, no airbrush, no glow. A fine even paper
grain over every surface of the whole image.
SKIN IS THE ONE EXCEPTION to the two-or-three-tones rule: each face and each hand is ONE
single flat tone, with no modelling, no blush, no shading, no cheekbones and no jawline.
On a face, draw ONLY these six things: the outline of the head, the eyes, the eyebrows,
the nose, the mouth and the ears. Nothing else at all — no line beside the nose, no line
under the eye, no shadow under the cheekbone, no shading on the neck or the hands.
HAIR IS ALWAYS A WARM DARK BROWN — #3f3330, its shadowed side #2e2523, its lit edge
#4d403c. NEVER grey, silver, white, ash, steel or blue-grey hair.

STRUCTURE — ONE single illustration, no panels and no dividing lines. WE ARE CLOSE IN, at
worktop height, in an ordinary home kitchen. TWO COOKING POTS STAND SIDE BY SIDE ON A
WOODEN WORKTOP IN THE MIDDLE OF THE FRAME, drawn large and seen from slightly above: they
are the same size and the same simple shape, with two small side handles each, and together
they fill roughly half the frame width and reach about two-thirds of the frame height.
Behind and above them a woman is reaching in towards them, her head tilted down, looking at
the two pots.

THE ONE THING THAT MAKES THIS PICTURE WORK — BOTH POTS HAVE BEEN KNOCKED IN EXACTLY THE
SAME PLACE, and the picture is about what each one shows when that happens. On BOTH pots,
on the upper rim on the side facing the middle of the picture, there is a damaged patch of
the same size and the same shape — an irregular bite out of the rim, about as wide as two
fingers, cutting into the outline of the pot so that the silhouette of the rim is visibly
broken there. Draw the two damaged patches at the same height and the same size, mirrored
towards each other, so a reader compares them instantly. THE DIFFERENCE IS WHAT IS
UNDERNEATH:
  • THE LEFT POT IS ONE SINGLE PIECE OF METAL — plain cast iron, the same dark warm
    grey-brown all over, inside and out, with a soft matte surface, no coating and no shine.
    Where its rim is knocked, THE METAL REVEALED IS EXACTLY THE SAME COLOUR AS THE REST OF
    THE POT — the damaged patch is almost invisible except for the dent in the outline and
    a slightly brighter scuff. It has been knocked, but nothing has come off it. Nothing
    lies on the worktop beneath it.
  • THE RIGHT POT IS ENAMELLED — a metal pot with a smooth glossy layer of pale warm
    off-white enamel fired onto it. Where its rim is chipped, A CLEARLY DARK PATCH IS
    EXPOSED, in strong contrast against the pale enamel around it — as dark as the left-hand
    pot, so the two read as the same metal. Around the edge of the chip, draw the enamel as
    a visible THIN LAYER seen edge-on, a fine pale band a millimetre or two thick sitting on
    top of the dark metal, so it is plain that this pot is two things and the outer one came
    off. Three or four broken pale enamel flakes lie on the worktop just below the chip, one
    of them large enough to see its curve, another lying on its edge.
Keep every other part of both pots clean and intact. The chip and the dent must both remain
readable when the whole picture is shrunk to the width of a thumbnail — that is why they
are on the rim and not on the body.

THE KITCHEN — an ordinary, warm, well-used Taiwanese home kitchen, plainly furnished and
clearly cooked in, but NOT cluttered near the pots: a wooden worktop, a tiled splashback
behind, a gas hob at one side with a kettle on it, a wooden chopping board standing on its
edge, a bowl of vegetables, a jar of cooking chopsticks and a ladle, a hanging cloth, a
small potted herb on the windowsill, a window at the left giving clean daylight. ONE lid
only, leaning upright against the left pot — do not put a second lid anywhere. The area of
worktop directly in front of and below the two pots is kept clear, so the enamel flakes are
the only thing on it. Do NOT add packaging, labelled bottles, appliances with control
panels or screens.

THE WOMAN — about forty, East Asian, seen from the chest up behind the worktop, her body
turned three-quarters towards the pots. Her head is tilted down and we see her face at a
three-quarter angle. She is looking at the chipped rim of the right-hand pot, not at the
viewer. Her expression is ordinary and unhurried — an everyday moment, mild recognition,
not worry and not surprise. The hand nearer the viewer is reaching towards the handle of
the left pot; her other arm rests along the worktop, the hand relaxed and drawn at a normal
size, not splayed and not larger than her face.

PEOPLE — Simplified illustrative people: minimal features (small dot or short-line eyes, a
simple line mouth, a small nose), natural realistic head-to-body proportions, a believable
age, an East Asian face. Not photorealistic. Not faceless. No oversized head, no noodle
limbs. She never looks at the viewer, and she is not taller than half the frame.
CLOTHING — a shirt in #b7c5d7 with its folds and shadows in #99a7b8, sleeves rolled up, and
a muted clay-rose apron over it. Clothes are NEVER flat single-tone shapes: give each
garment two or three tones of its own colour with soft coloured-pencil shading in the folds,
and draw the real details — collar, rolled cuff, apron strap and tie, a pocket.

LIGHT AND COLOUR — Ordinary bright indoor daytime, ONE light source: clean daylight from
the window on the left, warm and even. No long orange shadows, not sunset, not lamplight,
no spotlight on the pots. Across the upper part of the frame, above her and not touching
her, draw FOUR OR FIVE long, thin, white hand-drawn lines running the same way at the same
shallow angle, evenly spaced, solid where they begin and thinning to dry flecks — soft
chalk quality, clearly hand-made. They must never begin at her mouth, nose, hands or body,
must never cross her face, must never loop or curl back on themselves, and must never
resemble smoke, steam, cooking vapour or breath.
Most coloured areas sit in the range HSL saturation 30–50 and lightness 70–85 — clearly
coloured and readable, never washed out into near-white pastels and never heavy or muddy.
About half the picture is chromatic. Use AT LEAST FIVE clearly different colours and give
each to a named thing: the splashback tiles, the wooden worktop, her shirt, her apron, the
vegetables in the bowl, the potted herb, the kettle. Colour throughout — never greyscale.
ACCENT — #335b8b appears in exactly two small places and nowhere else: some of the
splashback tiles and one cupboard door below the worktop. Do NOT wash the image in that
colour, do NOT tint the skin, the pots, the worktop or the paper with it, and do NOT make
it vivid.

CRITICAL — NO WRITING ANYWHERE IN THE IMAGE. No text, letters, words, numbers, logos, brand
marks, packaging, labels, captions or watermarks, in any language or alphabet. The pots
carry no brand mark of any kind on the side, the handle, the rim or the lid. Jars, bottles,
tins, the kettle and the hob controls are completely blank. Where writing would normally
appear, leave the surface plain.

AVOID — greyscale; photorealism; faceless or noodle-limbed figures; thick uniform black
outlines; even mechanical vector linework; chrome, mirror-polished metal, metallic or
iridescent gradients; glow, sparkle or lens flare; grey, silver, white or blue-grey hair;
wrinkles, cheek shadow or facial modelling; an oversized or splayed hand; the chip drawn
small, faint, or in the middle of the pot's body instead of on the rim; the chip and the
dent placed at different heights or drawn at different sizes; the exposed metal in the chip
drawn pale, grey or the same colour as the enamel; any suggestion that one pot is more
expensive, newer or better than the other; a dirty, burnt, greasy or rusty kitchen; rust
running down either pot; food cooking, steam, flames or smoke; a recognisable branded or
designer pot; a second lid; clutter on the worktop in front of the pots; any arrow, tick,
cross, label or comparison mark between the two pots; teeth, mouths, gums or any dental
object anywhere in the picture; her looking at the viewer; a whole image tinted blue; flat
untextured single-tone clothing; a bare empty kitchen.
